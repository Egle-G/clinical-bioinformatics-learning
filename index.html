1 Why validate?

▼

Discussion

  • Why is validation necessary before a bioinformatics pipeline can be used in clinical service?
  • What could happen to a patient if a pipeline has not been properly validated?
  • What is the difference between validation and verification? When would each apply?
  • Who are the stakeholders in a validation process, and what are their responsibilities?
  • What published standards or guidelines would you refer to when planning a validation?

Think about how the following relate to validation:

  • Patient safety — false negatives could mean a pathogenic variant is missed; false positives could lead to unnecessary clinical interventions
  • Accreditation requirements — ISO 15189:2022 requires that examination procedures are validated before use. UKAS accreditation depends on this
  • Professional standards — the HCPC Standards of Proficiency require clinical scientists to understand the importance of quality assurance
  • Validation vs verification — validation confirms a method is fit for purpose; verification confirms an already-validated method performs as expected in your specific environment
  • Regulatory context — consider IVDR (In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation) implications for clinical software
The AMP/CAP 2018 guidelines (Roy et al.) provide 17 consensus recommendations for validating NGS bioinformatics pipelines. The ACGS NGS Bioinformatics Best Practice Guidelines (2016) provide UK-specific guidance including sensitivity thresholds and sample requirements.

2 Planning the validation

▼

Discussion

  • What would you need to agree with the genomics scientists before starting validation?
  • How would you determine what the pipeline should detect (scope of the assay)?
  • What types of variants should be included in a validation (think beyond just SNVs)?
  • How would you select appropriate validation samples? What characteristics should they have?
  • How many samples would you need, and how would you justify that number?
  • What is a "gold standard" in this context, and what options are available?

When planning validation, consider:

  • Requirements gathering — what variant types does the assay need to detect? What is the intended clinical application (rare disease, cancer, pharmacogenomics)?
  • Variant types — SNVs, indels, CNVs, structural variants, repeat expansions, mosaicism/somatic variants — each may require different validation approaches
  • Sample selection — reference materials (e.g. Genome in a Bottle/NIST, Horizon Discovery), previously characterised clinical samples, cell lines
  • Sample numbers — Consider the statistical basis for your chosen sample size to achieve required Confidence Intervals
  • Coverage and depth — what minimum coverage threshold is acceptable? How will you determine this? Consider downsampling experiments
  • Comparison method — orthogonal confirmation (e.g. Sanger sequencing), inter-laboratory comparisons, benchmarking datasets
The ACGS NGS Bioinformatics BPG states that pipeline sensitivity should be determined using clinical data, with resulting sensitivity having a 95% confidence interval greater than 0.95. Variants should be derived from at least 10 individuals.

3 Validation metrics and analysis

▼

Discussion

  • What metrics would you calculate to assess pipeline performance?
  • How would you determine appropriate quality thresholds for the new assay?
  • What is the limit of detection and why is it important — particularly for somatic assays?
  • How would you assess reproducibility? What factors could affect it?
  • How do confidence intervals relate to your validation results, and why do they matter?
  • What would you do if the pipeline did not meet the required performance thresholds?
  • Sensitivity (True Positive Rate) — probability that the test result will be positive when the variant is present. Sensitivity = a/(a+b) i.e. TP/(TP+FN). A missed variant could mean a missed diagnosis
  • Specificity (True Negative Rate) — probability that the test result will be negative when the variant is not present. Specificity = d/(c+d) i.e. TN/(TN+FP)
  • Precision (Positive Predictive Value) — probability that the variant is truly present when the test is positive. PPV = (Sensitivity × Prevalence) / (Sensitivity × Prevalence + (1−Specificity) × (1−Prevalence))
  • Negative Predictive Value (NPV) — probability that the variant is truly not present when the test is negative. NPV = (Specificity × (1−Prevalence)) / ((1−Sensitivity) × Prevalence + Specificity × (1−Prevalence))
  • Positive Likelihood Ratio (LR+) — ratio between the probability of a positive result given the presence of the variant and the probability of a positive result given its absence. LR+ = Sensitivity / (1−Specificity)
  • Negative Likelihood Ratio (LR−) — ratio between the probability of a negative result given the presence of the variant and the probability of a negative result given its absence. LR− = (1−Sensitivity) / Specificity
  • F1 Score — harmonic mean of precision and recall. F1 = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall). Balances both concerns
  • Accuracy — overall probability that a result is correctly classified. Accuracy = Sensitivity × Prevalence + Specificity × (1−Prevalence). Can be misleading with imbalanced classes
  • Confidence intervals — your sample size determines how confident you can be in your sensitivity/specificity estimates
  • Reproducibility — intra-run (same run, same sample), inter-run (different runs), inter-operator, inter-site consistency
  • Limit of detection — especially relevant for somatic variants: what is the lowest variant allele frequency the pipeline can reliably detect?
  • Quality thresholds — coverage depth, mapping quality, variant quality scores, genotype quality — how are thresholds set and justified?
  • Downsampling experiments — systematically reducing coverage to determine the minimum depth at which the pipeline still performs acceptably

4 Documentation and quality management

▼

Discussion

  • What documentation should be produced as part of the validation process?
  • How does validation documentation fit within a quality management system?
  • What change control processes should be in place when a validated pipeline is modified?
  • What ongoing quality assurance is needed after a pipeline enters clinical service?
  • How would you approach accreditation requirements (e.g. ISO 15189:2022, UKAS) in the context of a bioinformatics pipeline?
  • Validation report — documenting methods, results, conclusions, and any limitations
  • Standard operating procedures — for running the pipeline, interpreting outputs, and handling failures
  • User guides and training materials — for both bioinformaticians and the scientists who will use the outputs
  • Change control — any modification to a validated pipeline (software updates, reference data changes, parameter adjustments) requires documented assessment and potential revalidation
  • Version control — tracking pipeline versions, configurations, and dependencies (consider tools like Git, containerisation with Docker/Singularity)
  • Continuous validation — ongoing monitoring using benchmarking/reference samples run alongside clinical samples
  • EQA participation — external quality assessment schemes to verify ongoing performance
  • Audit trail — traceability from raw data through to reported results
ISO 15189:2022 requires medical laboratories to validate examination procedures, document validation results, and have procedures for ongoing quality assurance. The ACGS BPG recommends that bioinformatics pipelines should be treated as medical devices for quality management purposes.

5 Training, communication and go-live

▼

Discussion

  • Who needs to be trained before the pipeline goes into clinical use, and on what?
  • How would you communicate the pipeline's capabilities and limitations to the clinical team?
  • What steps are needed between completing validation and going live in clinical service?
  • What would you do if issues are discovered after the pipeline enters clinical use?
  • How does this process relate to your professional responsibility as a Clinical Scientist?
  • Training scope — bioinformaticians (running and maintaining the pipeline), laboratory scientists (understanding outputs and limitations), clinical staff (interpreting results in clinical context)
  • Competency documentation — evidence that users have been trained and assessed as competent
  • Communication — presenting validation results to stakeholders, explaining limitations clearly, agreeing turnaround times and escalation procedures
  • Go-live checklist — all documentation complete, training delivered, quality controls in place, SOPs approved, accreditation body notified if required
  • Post-implementation review — monitoring performance in the initial period, gathering user feedback, identifying issues early
  • Incident management — clear processes for reporting and investigating issues, including near-misses
  • Professional responsibility — HCPC Standards of Proficiency, duty to raise concerns, knowing the limits of your competence

7 Knowledge Check

▼

Test your understanding with these self-assessment questions. These are not FYA questions — they are designed to help you identify gaps in your knowledge.

Q1. You have completed a validation study for a new NGS bioinformatics pipeline. Which of the following best describes what should be included in the validation documentation?

Correct. Validation documentation should be comprehensive and traceable. It includes the validation report itself (covering methods, samples used, results, sensitivity/specificity, limitations, and conclusions), but also the supporting documentation needed before the pipeline can enter clinical service — SOPs for operation, evidence that users have been trained and assessed as competent, and documented change control approval. This forms part of the laboratory's quality management system and is required for accreditation.
Not quite. Validation documentation needs to be comprehensive and fit within the laboratory's quality management system. A results summary alone is insufficient — the documentation should enable another scientist to understand exactly what was tested, how, and why. It should also include the operational documentation (SOPs, training records, change control) needed to demonstrate the pipeline is ready for clinical use. Think about what an auditor or accreditation assessor would expect to see.

Q2. A bioinformatics pipeline has been validated and is in clinical use. A software dependency is updated to a new version. What should happen next?

Correct. Any modification to a validated pipeline requires a documented change control assessment. The impact of the change should be evaluated to determine whether full revalidation, partial revalidation, or verification testing is needed. This is a key quality management requirement.
Not quite. Software updates — even minor ones — can affect pipeline outputs. A change control process should assess the impact and determine the appropriate level of revalidation or verification needed. Think about what could change when a dependency is updated.

Q3. You are selecting samples for validating a pipeline. Which approach best meets published guidelines?

Correct. The ACGS guidelines recommend variants from at least 10 individuals. A combination of reference materials (e.g. GIAB/NIST) and clinical samples provides both well-characterised truth sets and clinically relevant variant representation. The samples should cover the full range of variant types the pipeline is expected to detect.
Not quite. Published guidelines recommend using clinical data from at least 10 individuals. A single reference sample or purely computational variants would not adequately validate clinical performance. Consider why diversity of samples and variant types matters for a robust validation.

Q4. You are validating a pipeline and need to determine the limit of detection (LOD). What does the limit of detection represent in the context of an NGS bioinformatics pipeline?

Correct. The limit of detection is the lowest variant allele frequency (VAF) at which the pipeline can reliably identify a variant. This is particularly critical for somatic pipelines where tumour heterogeneity and sample purity mean variants may be present at low allele frequencies, but it is also relevant for germline pipelines when considering mosaicism or low-quality samples. Determining LOD typically involves serial dilution or in silico downsampling experiments.
Not quite. The limit of detection refers to the lowest variant allele frequency at which the pipeline can reliably call a variant. This is distinct from coverage depth (which affects LOD but is not the same thing) and from sample size requirements. Understanding LOD is important because it defines the boundary of what your pipeline can and cannot detect — variants below this threshold may be missed, with direct implications for patient care.

Q5. Which quality standard requires medical laboratories to validate examination procedures before clinical use?

Correct. ISO 15189:2022 (Medical laboratories — Requirements for quality and competence) is the standard that requires medical laboratories to validate examination procedures and is the basis for UKAS accreditation of clinical laboratories in the UK.
Not quite. ISO 15189:2022 is the relevant standard for medical laboratory quality and competence. ISO 27001 relates to information security, ISO 9001 to general quality management, and ISO 14001 to environmental management. Understanding which standards apply to clinical laboratory work is part of professional foundations.

Standards
